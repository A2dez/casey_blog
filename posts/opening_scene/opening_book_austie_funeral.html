2.0.0.1 A double blow

Easter Monday, March 26, 1951

It must have been 10 o’clock in the morning when Dr Anne Casey, 36, hauled herself out of the bed, worried sick. She had taken to the bed with pregnancy complications and didn’t want to be getting up yet. She was due any day now. She waddled out of the room, past the huge sash window in the corridor and into the mirror-image bedroom occupied by her husband, Dr Michael Augustine Casey, 52.

Austie Casey hadn’t been in the best of health for some time and he’d already had two heart attacks. As soon as Anne opened the door, she let out a yelp, to the horror of the children and anyone else scattered around the house. There he was, dead to the world. He had slipped away in his sleep, painlessly she hoped.

The big house was curiously empty for a festive weekend. We don’t know what her physical difficulties were. Oedema, perhaps, or maybe toxemia. But things were definitely not looking good for the bambino. Laura Ann had been a miracle and was a ruddy two year old now, the first Rhesus baby in Ireland to survive. She got the all-important blood transfusion, a procedure that had only recently been developed in America. Austie Casey must have had to muscle his way through his medical contacts in Dublin as he managed to pull together a team who could save the baby. The chances of survival would be even lower for this bambino but there was still hope. Being so ill herself, and with her husband gone, Anne didn’t have either the energy or the clout to pull together the right team for the coming birth. Alas, nine days later, the young widow would bear a still born baby, Joseph Patrick Casey, 0, and his little body would be buried beside his father in the loose soil of the family grave.

Jane was only four yet and remembers so much. How could you not? Richard was six and Michael, the eldest, was eight, his father’s pride and joy. Now he would have to become the man of the house. Eventually, once the initial shock wore off and Anne came to she told Michael to run down and get the nanny. When no one came, Anne had to traipse down the stairs, her flock trailing behind her, to ask the maids where that aul wan was gone to. They said she was gone to Mass. The aul dragon! She’s always gone to mass when she’s wanted! Anne struggled back up the stairs and Michael went in with his mammy to see his father, and him there, gone.

2.0.0.2 Enjoy it while you can

(The next two paragraphs will likely be expanded into whole chapters, and may not be needed here).

The early years had been idyllic for the Casey family. The war was over, the world was opening back up, and the prosperous couple had begun to galavant. Austie Casey had, since his appointment in 1932, been the Medical Officer for the county town of Dungarvan. He was a dispensary doctor and Anne, who didn’t have a post yet herself, helped make up the medicines, freeing Austie from that drudgery. It was time he did not waste. Incredibly, he was also the managing director of the town’s two factories, both of which he had set up. In recent years, he found himself and his beautiful wife travelling all around America, buying machines and visiting family in Chicago and Los Angeles. Business was booming now and the men of Dungarvan had steady work, all year round, largely thanks to Austie Casey. The 1950s hadn’t happened yet and the mood in Ireland was still hopeful. No sooner were the lovers back from The States and they went on a cruise to Portugal and Madeira. They had a nanny and three maids to help them raise the children, along with Granny May, Anne’s mother.

After the tragedy, the four surviving children lived like stray cats, as Laura Ann remembers it, at least until the evening when they’d be home for their daily roast dinner. The older ones had more responsibility and had to face the horror head on. With no father, their mother would be flat out as a dispensary doctor, out in Gaeltacht area of Ring and Old Parish, working like a dog to keep the whole thing going. She only got a paltry stipend in accordance with Austie’s second will, which had been changed after one of his heart attacks. Soon enough, Austie Casey’s siblings all began to drop, some of them afflicted by the same heart condition. In ten years’ time, only one of the six was still alive. JOE?

2.1 Dungarvan’s leading townsman

“The Casey name would be forever be linked with the industrial revival in Dungarvan.’, Tom Kyne, T.D. 1957.

The following day, at noon, an emergency meeting was convened Dungarvan Urban District Council, to cast a vote of sympathy for their leading townsman. Dungarvan is a small but historically self-sufficient town along the south coast of Ireland, thirty miles west of Waterford City and fifty miles east of Cork City. In the 1946 census, it had a population of 5,276 people, but a large hinterland, and it was the seat of the Waterford county council, distinct then from the city council. Dungarvan’s geography is remarkably similar to that of Dublin. It sits at the centre of a large, protected bay with a river splitting it into two unequal parts. To the north is the village and parish of Abbeyside while Dungarvan is to the south of the River Colligan, a fast-flowing river that rushes down from the mountains inland, albeit to the north. The farm land is very good in the hinterlands but there is no deepwater port so it never grew into a major trading post. Fishing has always been important in the area but even that began to dwindle in the early twentieth century. So in the 1930s, when Fianna Fail decided to dot factories all over the country, in an effort to make the Irish Free State a self-sufficient economy, the Dungarvan Industrial Development Committee, chaired by Fr Tommy Walsh, and led by Dr Michael Augustine Casey, managed to convince the government the Dungarvan was the perfect location for a tannery. With a river to provide clean water in, and the sea to wash away the noxious waste, its somewhat derelict quays were an ideal spot to locate the factory, despite the smell that would pervade the town for decades to come.

At the meeting, effusive tributes poured forth from several members present. While they recognised Dr Casey’s considerate and painstaking work as a doctor in the town, they mostly saw him as their leading townsman and captain of local industry, the greatest industrialist Dungarvan has ever known. The man who was responsible for the town’s industrial revival, with two flourishing factories. He gave employment, all the year round, to hundreds of men from the town and surrounding countryside, at the best of wages. Without Dr Casey, the town would not be as prosperous, as busy, or even as big as it is. He had been responsible for ending emigration from the town. By the time of the funeral, the following morning, Dr Casey had become perhaps the greatest industrialist in Munster, if not the country. Filling his shoes would be most difficult, if not impossible.

It was noted that very few people realise the strenuous work that Austie had put into the starting of the two factories. His behind the scenes work would not have been visible to the workers and ordinary folk in the town. This contemporary insight is crucial for our understanding of Austie Casey’s role in establishing Dickens Leather factory back in 1935. Without it, one might argue that Austie Casey wasn’t the greatest industrialist in even Dungarvan, as there were two bona fide leather men higher up the chain in that factory when it was established. The three of them demonstrate the three main groups who took part in Ireland’s economic revolution in the 1930s. Austie Casey was one of the ‘ruddy-faced gombeen men’ of rural Ireland who oversaw the localization of industry around the country. There were the English capitalists looking to get into the newly protected Irish markets, and there were the Dublin-based professional class who liaised between the Brits and the provincial Irish.

Kennedy O’Brien, a Limerick man based in Dublin, would, over his lifetime, build a small empire across the South-East of Ireland, producing leathers and related products. He set up a hard leathers tannery in nearby Portlaw, making hard soles from cow hides, and Dungarvan was set up a year later as a complementary operation, tanning soft leathers from sheep hides for the softer, upper parts of the shoe. He was the managing director in Portlaw and the first Chairman in Dickens Leather.

Reginald Dickens, who gave his name to the Dungarvan tannery, hailed from Northampton, the heart of leather production in England and indeed the world. He was the managing director of the company. Dickens’ father had set up a tannery around the turn of the century and it was taken over by the elder brothers. Reginald was a younger son who had to go and seek his fortune. When the newly formed Irish Free State began to place tariffs on imported goods, hoping to become a self-sufficient state, Reginald was among a band of opportunistic English industrialists who set up shop over here, in adherence with Fianna Fail’s jealously regulated isolationist policies. Hoops had to be jumped through and loopholes had to be found, and no one was better at this than the lawyer, Arthur Cox, and the accountant Vincent Crowley, who was Chairman in Portlaw and on the first board of Dickens Leather.

The genius of Austie Casey was in getting a factory to Dungarvan at all, a task he began through the local Industrial Development Committee, alongside Fr Tommy Walsh. A few weeks’ later, at the Dickens Leather AGM, Fr Walsh would summarise Austie’s motivation for becoming an industrialist. He “was particularly noted for his devotion the very poor among his clientele. It was his painful experiences of the baneful effects and ravages of unemployment and its consequent poverty and misery, that provided him with a powerful incentive to embark on his industrial activities, and thus remedy as far as he could those crying evils. Also like every loyal son of Ireland he was anxious to play his part in the re-birth of our nations, and the revival of her industries.”

Once the factory was in the town Austie Casey could muscle his way to the top and go on to set up a second, offshoot factory. While he couldn’t bring the knowledge of an industry insider like Dickens and O’Brien, Austie Casey had the contacts in the Fianna Fail government of 1932 to convince them to give Dungarvan a factory. He knew Kennedy O’Brien through his Waterford circles, but he may well have known Lemass from his time in the IRA.

2.1.1 Mountain View House

  • Beyond, there’s an entry area with wood floor, and just beyond a stone floor section running the rest of the way through the rather deep property.

  • The staff would have tended to the ample gardens which were walled off from the surroundings, leaving an idyllic space for Austie’s young children to wander and play. They might enter in the back door

Austie Casey’s body was due to be removed on the Tuesday evening to the parish church nearby. Mr and Mrs Casey lived in a large Georgian house called Mountain View, on a two-acre plot just off O’Connell Street, as you head west out from the square, a few hundred metres. Despite being so close to the town centre, Mountain View feels like a country house. Out the back were ample walled gardens with apple trees and plenty more. The front of the house was then covered in ivy, head to toe, and there was a beautiful ornate porch which has sadly been removed. The original residents, in 1815, were the Dower family, local scions of commerce and law. Eventually it would come to house the local Medical Officer and, in its fine original state, it had space aplenty for a family doctor’s duties by day and entertaining guests by night, with seven bedrooms, a twenty-six-foot long dining room, separated by a large arch from a fine drawing room with a huge fireplace. Upstairs, to the front of the house, are matching master bedrooms, with huge sash windows looking straight out at Cruachan in the nearby Comeragh Mountains. The rooms face north, making them slightly dreary, and leaving poor Austie and Ann to suffer in the absence of restorative light.

So when Austie Casey was appointed to the role of Medical Officer in Dungarven, Mountain View House became the incongruously large home of a single man in his early thirties, at a time when young Anne Foley was still in Ardmore, studying for the Leaving Certificate. Since the house came with the job, Austie had never actually bought it, which was bad news for the widowed Anne. Instead, his money went into the factory.

A big family

Possible discussion of Grawn here.

Percy would have been the first of Austie’s five surviving siblings to get to Mountain View. He was Austie’s younger brother and had become is right hand man. It was he who would try to take on the job of filling Austie’s boots. Percy lived on the main street in Dungarvan and still oversaw the family business, a shop that had been owned by their father, Michael J., the much-loved champion of the town’s poor, until his death in 1923, and later taken over by his equally-loved wife, Catherine, until her death in 1940. Both of them had huge funerals in the town. Together the Casey brothers set up the Glue and Gelatine factory during the war. Percy would become managing director of Dickens Leather and would begin to build business connections abroad. Like their father, Percy would become a member of the county council before himself succumbing to heart failure six years later, also leaving behind a widow and some very young children.

The rest of the siblings were as scattered as any large Irish family of the time. Cork, Dublin, England, America. May was married to a Dr Michael O’Shea, and they lived in his native Cork City. Fr Rennie, short for Lawrence, had come from Lancashire where he worked as part of a mission among the large Irish community, although he would retire with illness and see out his days in Mountain View, later that decade. Joe was so long gone from Dungarvan that the council failed to mention his name in their offering their condolences to Austie’s family. ELABORATE. He was now a highly successful accountant, and had flown all the way from Los Angeles, where Austie and Anne had visited him on their tour of the States.

The eldest of the family was the formidable character of the Right Reverand Monsignor Richard J. Casey, a fascinating character if ever there was one. He was ordained in the 1914 and immediately posted to New York where he brought huge energy to his parish. First he brought parishioners back in to the congregation, and the church’s marching brass band would be known as Fr Casey’s band. Most importantly, however, he became a huge advocate of the Irish revolutionary cause and would give powerful talks to huge audiences from the diaspora, raising both awareness and funding. It was this ability to crowdsource money that would prove so beneficial to Austie when it came to raising money for a factory in Dungarvan. Just before Independence, Dick was posted back to Ireland and in 1923 he became a chaplain in the Army. He got in at a good time and rose to head chaplain in the 1940s. In 1948, Dick took active retirement, by his standards, as a Parish Priest in Skerries in North County Dublin, a role he kept until his death in 1960. He was a regular guest in Mountain View and his nieces all remember him well. It is highly unlikely, given his profession, that he was at home in Dungarvan for Easter when Austie died. He would surely have driven down from Skerries. He loved driving. He even brought his car home from New York in 1920 when his time there had come to an end.

2.2 Removal

To keep them occupied, he’d set them tasks like jumping to reach a target on the wall and giving them money for it after.

The house was thronged for the removal. Despite its size, Mountain View would not have been equipped to deal with the sheer number of people who wanted to pay their respects to their deceased talisman. The shutters would have been closed and the servants would have been making tea and sandwiches all day long, and bringing them from the kitchen quarters in the back out to the drawing room where we can imagine a rotating crew of mourners in disbelief. How could Austie Casey have been taken so young? On the lovely mahogany table were cakes to ease the pain. Lent was over now and everything would have tasted extra good. Mooching about the house were Austie’s four young children. Jane recalls rummaging through the shutters to see if she could find any more sweets. Daddy always bought them sweets but rationed them out, two at a time.

We might imagine Austie’s body, dressed in a dark suit, having been laid out in the dining room for people to pay their last respects. They would have come and gone all day long but in the evening was the removal. Employees of Austie’s two factories shouldered the coffin in relays all the way from Mountain View to St Mary’s Church, fully five hundred metres. Other workers from the firms acted as a guard of honour while hundreds of people lined the sidewalk and paid their respects to the huge cortege behind. The slightly shorter route would have been out the back of Mountain View which then had access to Mitchell Street, but it seems more likely they went along O’Connell St to the square and then turned right up towards the church. There the body was received by a flock of priests, from every parish around, and many beyond. To the east and north were priests from Kilgobnet, Abbeyside, Stradbally, Ballinameela, Kilrossanty, Touraneena, Fourmilewater, Rathgormac(k), Portlaw, Carrick-on-Suir, and of course Tommy Walsh who was now in St Patrick’s Church in Waterford City. From the west and south were priests from Carriglea, Ring and Old Parish, Ardmore, Clashmore, and Cappoquin. Other priests came from Kilkenny, England and Scotland.

2.3 Funeral

Dungarvan shut down on the morning of the funeral and the place was so jammed that the car couldn’t move through the crowd. Despite its size, the church couldn’t accommodate all the mourners and hundreds had to stand in the precincts. Everyone could attend the burial immediately afterwards as the family plot was right by the entrance to the church. There would be huge overlap in the attendees of the removal on the Tuesday night and the burial on the Wednesday morning, and the people listed demonstrate the remarkably wide range of activities and contacts Austie had made in a life that paralleled perfectly the Irish nation in the first half of the twentieth century.

A very large number in attendance would have been patients of Austie, for whom he had worked so painstakingly and conscientiously since his appointment as the local medical officer in 1932, after a stint in nearby Cappoquin. There were the factory staff who had carried his body to the church the night before, and a huge number of locals there to pay their respects to such an important figure in the town.

Beyond that, the gathering was a diffuse, educated bunch, many of them leaders in the newly formed republic, hailing mostly from the East and South of the country. There were Austie’s medical contacts, built up first in University College Dublin, where his studies overlapped with the Revolutionary Period of 1916-23, what he would have called The Troubles. His self-confessed involvement with the IRA in both Dublin and Waterford got him involved in politics and, in particular, Fianna Fail after their establishment and rise to power. It was through these contacts that, in 1935, he could organise the establishment of a large leather factory in Dungarvan, which Minister Sean Lemass would open. Several members attended from Dail Eireann and Seanad Eireann, along with officials from all public bodies in the country. Members of the Urban District Council attended as a single bloc, an agreement made at their emergency meeting on the Tuesday.

As a board member of two factories, Austie came into contact with many members of the legal and banking professions, and other managers and directors of various industrial concerns throughout the provinces of Munster and Leinster. It was through the work of such men that Ireland tried to fashion itself as a self-sufficient state, independent of Britain and scarcely connected by trade to Europe. There were leaders of the AT&GWU union with whom Austie had come into contact during the strike of 1948. Among the leaders in education were An Fear Mór and members of the nearby Vocational School who had trained so many workers in the factory.

Austie’s involvement in Gaelic games was honoured by the President of the GAA and very large attendance from the Gaels of Waterford, namely the people of the GAA community. Austie played a huge role in local games. This era was the high point of Waterford hurling and football. They won the All-Ireland hurling final in 1948 and 1949, with Dungarvan being the bulk suppliers, and even the Waterfod Football team won…

In attendance also were also Garda and Army representatives in attendance;, including the FCA. Some of these may have gotten to know Austie through Dick’s contacts as Army chaplain. Astonishingly, Dick was the celebrant of the mass (a Solemn Requiem and High mass) and then the burial too which took place in the family plot right outside the church door, as befitted a man of his stature, and his father before him who had been in a member of the local council for so many years. Austie would be buried beside their little brother, John, who was only a few years younger than him but who had died aged 11, in 1917. The first Michael died in infancy in 1892. Austie’s children did not attend the solemnities. Two lovely girls from the factory came to mind them in the drawing room and no doubt they gobbled up any leftover cakes. But Jane just wanted her mammy, and soon the four surviving children were all that Anne would have.
